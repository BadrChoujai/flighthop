Scope

What ships, and in what order

The phases are a real sequence: each one is only worth building once the previous one is trustworthy. Shipping alerts before the layover maths is correct just means notifying people about connections they will miss.

V1

The impossible route

One origin, one destination — airport or country — a date range, one stop. This alone is a product.

  • Self-connect search with UTC-correct layover maths
  • Airport or country as destination
  • Ranking by price plus comfort, with the weights exposed as sliders
  • Per-leg deep links into Ryanair's own booking flow
  • Month view for flexible dates
  • A plain-language warning that two tickets are two tickets
V2

Discovery and confidence

Turn a search tool into somewhere to browse, and make the risky results honest.

  • Anywhere under €X, filtered by country, region and departure time
  • Risk badge: layover length, bag re-check, terminal change, border crossing, last-flight-of-the-day
  • Backup flight: for tight connections, the next departure and what rebooking costs
  • Weekend break mode built on the round-trip endpoint
  • Bag and seat costs added per leg, so the total is comparable to a legacy fare
V3

Coming back to it

  • Saved searches with price-drop and new-route alerts
  • Two-stop routing, geographically pruned, only when one stop finds nothing
  • Passport-aware filtering — hide hubs the traveller cannot transit
  • Map view of the route graph, because the graph is genuinely fun to explore
  • Second carrier (Wizz, easyJet) — the algorithm never cared which airline

Visual direction

Night apron

Airline sites shout in primary colours; this one is a tool for reading timetables, so it borrows from the things that already do that well — departure boards, aeronautical charts, transit diagrams. Cool blue-grey neutrals, a single teal that stands for flying, and a sodium amber that stands for waiting. That pairing is the whole interface: every itinerary is teal, amber, teal.

Rules the palette has to follow

  • Teal is the only accent. Everything clickable is teal; nothing decorative is.
  • Amber is not a second brand colour — it means waiting, and waiting is also the risk axis, which is why the layover bar and the "tight connection" chip share it. That overlap is the point.
  • Green, amber and red never appear alone. Every risk state carries a word as well as a hue, because a colour-blind traveller still has a plane to catch.
  • Neutrals are biased cool, toward the accent. A pure grey next to teal reads as a mistake.

Type

Two voices. Interface text in the platform's own UI face, which is quiet and reads well at small sizes. Everything that is data — times, prices, IATA codes, durations — in a monospace with tabular figures, so digits line up in a column and the eye can scan a page of departures the way it scans a departure board.

Interface

Three fields, then get out of the way

The search is one line because the interesting decisions happen after the results appear, not before. "Anywhere" and a country name are first-class inputs, not an advanced mode.

Search
To Accepts an airport, a city, a country, or the word Anywhere. Same field, no mode switch.
When A whole month is a valid answer. Flexibility is the single biggest lever on price, so the UI never forces an exact date.
Stops Not in the search bar. Default to one stop, offer two only when one finds nothing under budget.

Anatomy of a result

  • Price first, on the left, in mono at 21px — it is what the eye goes to, and column alignment lets you compare down the list without reading.
  • The timeline is the result, not an illustration of it. Bar width is elapsed time, so a nine-hour trip is visibly longer than a six-hour one before any number is read.
  • The gap is drawn, not described. Hatched amber says "you are sitting in a terminal" faster than "7h 18m layover" ever will.
  • Both clocks are shown. Local time at each airport, because that is what the boarding pass says, with the honest total computed underneath in UTC.
  • Risk is a chip with a word in it — comfortable tight risky — placed next to the gap it describes.
  • Booking is two buttons, deliberately. Two tickets means two transactions; hiding that behind one "Book" button would be a lie with a €200 consequence.

Filters live beside the results, not in a modal

Every filter re-ranks the list in place. The sliders that matter — minimum layover, maximum price, latest arrival — are the ones from the ranking formula, so the user is tuning the same knobs the algorithm uses. No hidden second model.

The states that decide whether it feels good

Searching

A graph search takes seconds. Show hub names as they resolve — "checking Bergamo, Barcelona…" — so the wait reads as work, not as a hang.

Nothing under your budget

Never a dead end. Offer the two nearest exits: the cheapest result that does exist, and the same trip with two stops allowed.

Route has vanished

Seasonal routes disappear mid-planning. Say which leg died and what it costs now, then re-run the search around it.

Self-transfer notice

Shown once per session, near the booking buttons, in plain words: if the first flight is late, nobody owes you the second one.

On a phone

The timeline survives the narrow column — it is the one element worth keeping at full width. Price moves above the bar, the leg details collapse to origin and destination with times, and the filter rail becomes a bottom sheet that keeps the result list visible behind it while you drag.

Build

How it holds together

The fare endpoints refuse browser calls from another origin, so the search runs on the server. That is also where the cache belongs, and the cache is most of the architecture.

LayerChoiceWhy
AppNext.js or SvelteKitServer routes double as the proxy the CORS rules force you to have.
CachePostgres or SQLite, keyed (from, to, month)Airport table: weeks. Route graph: days. Month fares: hours. Live search then answers in under a second.
WorkerBackground refresh of popular routesAlso powers price-drop alerts — the API hands you the previous price for free.
MannersConcurrency ≤ 4, backoff on 403/429Undocumented endpoints on a real airline's infrastructure. Two of them already return 409 and 403 to naive callers.

Before it becomes a business

Ryanair has litigated hard against automated access to its fares. Unauthenticated endpoints and public data do not override the site terms, and personal tooling sits in a very different position from a commercial product. If this ever charges money, the honest routes are a distribution agreement or a licensed aggregator feed — not scaling this quietly.
